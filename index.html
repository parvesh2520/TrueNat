    The idea

    A PCR lab, shrunk to a lunchbox.

    Conventional real-time PCR needs a thermal cycler, a fluorescence reader and a trained team in a reference laboratory. Truenat moves the hardest part — fast, precise heating — onto a disposable chip, so the analyzer around it only has to power, cool and watch.

    02

    Under the shell

    Eleven parts, one job.

    Fade the enclosure away and the layout appears: a chip bay under a light-tight lid, an optical module looking up at the reaction, a fan and heat sink behind it, a battery, and the controller board that runs it all.

    03

    The micro-PCR chip

    The chip heats itself.

    Freeze-dried primers, probes and polymerase wait inside the reaction chamber. Add 6 µL of extracted DNA and a thin-film heater printed around the chamber cycles it between ~95 °C and ~60 °C — doubling the target sequence every cycle.

    04

    Real-time detection

    Light says the DNA is there.

    After every cycle an LED excites the fluorescent probes. Each copy of the target cuts a probe, separating reporter from quencher, so the glow grows with the DNA. A photodetector logs it, cycle by cycle, into an amplification curve.

        08 — Reading the result

        The cycle that crosses the line.

        Fluorescence stays at background until enough copies exist to be seen. The cycle at which the curve crosses the threshold is the Ct value: the more target DNA in the sample, the earlier it crosses.

        A second dye tracks an internal positive control. If the control does not amplify, the run is invalid — protecting against false negatives from failed extraction or inhibitors.
